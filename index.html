Northern Pathways

A proposed home education and wellbeing centre in Richmond, NSW, supporting families of students with disability. I am Nathaniel Hammill, a preservice primary teacher. My aim is to help families build an education program that is safe, purposeful and connected to the local community.

Why the centre exists

Home-schooling registrations in NSW have more than doubled since 2019, and many children moving from public schools into home schooling have disability recorded in school data (Audit Office of NSW, 2025). Northern Pathways responds by combining curriculum planning with community participation and wellbeing support.

What families can expect

I use Maslow’s hierarchy as a planning lens: physical and sensory needs, safety, belonging, confidence and future goals all matter alongside academic learning. The centre does not replace qualified health professionals; it helps families connect education with appropriate local services.

WHAT? · TAB 1

What can home schooling offer students with disability?

Northern Pathways is a proposed Richmond, NSW centre with a physical base so families can meet, access resources and take part in small-group learning without relying only on the family home. NSW guidance allows parents to adapt learning to a child’s individual needs while still working from NSW syllabuses.

Three initiatives: (1) low-cost learning profiles that identify strengths, interests, adjustments and suitable ways to show learning; (2) bespoke community learning, such as museum, gallery, park and aquatic-centre visits linked to syllabus outcomes; and (3) family navigation sessions covering registration, curriculum planning and referrals to appropriate local disability or wellbeing services.

These initiatives matter because flexibility can reduce sensory or attendance pressure, but home schooling can also narrow access to specialist teaching and peers if families are left to manage alone (Paulauskaite et al., 2022). My role is therefore to support flexibility without allowing it to become isolation.

WHAT? · TAB 2

What support can agencies offer?

Northern Pathways would collaborate with headspace Hawkesbury for youth mental-health support, Autism Spectrum Australia (Aspect) for autism-specific education and family guidance, and Hawkesbury City Council for inclusive community, library and recreation opportunities. Contact would be made through each organisation’s published service team rather than inventing named staff members.

A regular week could use Richmond Library, Hawkesbury Regional Gallery, Hawkesbury Regional Museum, Oasis Aquatic and Leisure Centre, Richmond Park and local medical/allied-health services. Families can plan routes through Transport for NSW.

Other useful counselling or psychology pathways include headspace, Kids Helpline, Relationships Australia NSW and the Australian Psychological Society’s Find a Psychologist service. For students with disability, wellbeing is strengthened when education includes belonging, predictable relationships and meaningful community participation rather than focusing only on completed schoolwork.

WHAT? · TAB 3

Why has home schooling increased?

Home schooling is not new, but COVID-19 made learning at home familiar to many families and exposed gaps in adjustments for some students with disability. During shutdowns, Disability Royal Commission evidence described students missing accessible teaching, individual support and peer contact.

NSW registrations rose from 5,906 in 2019 to 12,762 in 2024. The Audit Office also reported that, among registered home-schooled children who had previously attended NSW public schools, the estimated proportion recorded with disability increased from about 35% to 54% across that period.

Media accounts show that families may choose home schooling after anxiety, school refusal or repeated experiences of exclusion. Maslow helps explain why: if safety and belonging are not secure, participation in learning can become difficult. However, moving learning home does not automatically meet these needs; families still need deliberate peer, community and professional connections.

RECENT PRESSURES ON PARENT CHOICE

Why some families may look beyond a standard school model

Guardian Australia · August 2026

Leah: when a flexible program disappears

Guardian Australia reported on Leah, a Year 8 student with complex medical needs who had been able to return from hospital and continue learning through a self-directed Montessori stream. Her mother said the flexibility reduced anxiety because Leah did not have to re-enter a class that had moved far ahead while she was absent. The stream is now being phased out as Victorian schools move towards a stronger emphasis on explicit instruction.

For this website, the important point is not that explicit instruction is “bad”. It is that one teaching approach will not suit every learner in every situation. When parents see a program that works for their child disappear, home schooling or another flexible arrangement can become more attractive because it gives them greater control over pace, method and continuity.

ABC News · August 2026

Tommy: exclusion when a setting cannot meet disability-related needs

ABC News reported that four-year-old Tommy Schumacher, who has Perthes disease and uses a wheelchair, was excluded from his early learning centre after the service said its change area could no longer safely accommodate his disability-related needs. His mother described the decision as abrupt, and the family had not found another centre able to take him.

Tommy is not a home-schooling case, but his experience shows why parents of children with disability may lose confidence in mainstream services when reasonable access depends on facilities, staffing or funding that are not available. A home-based option may feel safer or more controllable, although it also transfers significant responsibility back to the family.

SO WHAT? · TAB 4

What are the concerns about home schooling?

Educational disadvantage: parents may not have the specialist knowledge, equipment, time or income needed to reproduce the support available in a well-resourced school. Research with families of children with neurodevelopmental conditions identified access to resources, social activities and specialist help as practical barriers (Paulauskaite et al., 2022).

Safeguarding: fewer routine contacts with teachers and professionals can reduce opportunities for concerns about physical or sexual abuse to be noticed or disclosed. This is a visibility issue, not evidence that home schooling itself causes abuse; NSW Parliamentary research has noted the lack of evidence for a simple causal claim.

Social isolation: peer contact no longer happens automatically and must be planned. News reporting on students with disability has shown how exclusion from school can leave young people spending long periods at home without a stable alternative.

The Disability Royal Commission’s education hearings are relevant here. In the case of “Sam”, repeated suspension was followed by home schooling, meaning he lost professional teaching and everyday opportunities to form peer relationships. The broader Commission evidence also links exclusion, inadequate adjustments and low expectations with long-term effects on education, mental health and adult pathways. For me, this means home schooling needs protective community contact, not just a change of location.

SO WHAT? · TAB 5

What policies support home schooling?

Under the Education Act 1990 (NSW), registered home schooling is a legitimate way to meet compulsory education requirements. NSW guidance also allows children with disability or additional learning needs to be registered, with parents adapting the program while using relevant NSW syllabuses.

A weakness is that a compliant plan on paper does not guarantee high-quality teaching every day. The Audit Office of NSW identified limitations in historical monitoring of program quality and student progress, creating scope for poor provision to go unnoticed.

Nationally, the Disability Standards for Education 2005 provide a useful benchmark for access, participation, consultation and reasonable adjustment. Internationally, Article 24 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities recognises equal access to education. Neither framework specifically promotes home schooling; both reinforce the child’s right to meaningful participation.

SO WHAT? · TAB 6

How can parents enrich home schooling?

Parents often know their child’s triggers, interests, communication preferences and local community better than anyone else. This can support genuinely tailored learning: a child who struggles with noise may learn in shorter sessions, while a strong interest can be used to build literacy, numeracy or inquiry tasks. Research identifies personalisation and one-to-one support as possible strengths of home education, although these need to be balanced with specialist advice and social opportunities (Paulauskaite et al., 2022).

ABC News reported on Fletcher, an autistic child whose mother used his interest in pop music to increase engagement with learning. The case also highlighted the need to maintain opportunities for social interaction outside the home.

NOW WHAT? · TAB 7

Two bespoke case studies

Gemma’s daughter

Guardian Australia reported that Gemma Troughton moved to home schooling after her autistic daughter experienced severe anxiety around school. Immediate concerns were emotional safety and re-engagement; longer-term concerns include friendships and independence. Two practical challenges are the loss of family income when a parent stops work and the need to organise peer contact.

I would recommend an OT-informed sensory plan, psychological support for anxiety and one regular interest-based social activity. Future planning would gradually include independent travel, self-advocacy and community recreation so home schooling does not narrow adult options.

Taylor Lloyd

Guardian Australia reported that Taylor’s family chose home schooling after difficulties obtaining the inclusive mainstream education they wanted for him. Immediate concerns were meaningful communication and learning; longer-term concerns include social life, accommodation choices and adult participation. Challenges include replacing specialist expertise and avoiding a social world made up mostly of family and therapists.

I would recommend AAC/speech pathology support, accessible recreation and a physiotherapy-informed movement routine. Longer-term learning would include decision-making, community access and supported work or volunteering connected to Taylor’s interests.

NOW WHAT? · TAB 8

Contact with local schools

Northern Pathways maintains contact with local schools so students can keep pathways to peers, specialist staff, sport, wellbeing services and future re-entry open. In NSW, registered home schooling is not automatically a legal part-time public-school arrangement, so any hybrid approach would need to fit current enrolment and school policy rather than being promised to families.

Useful local-school supports include learning and support teams, school sport and physical activity, and counselling/wellbeing services. A benefit of a carefully negotiated mixed model is that it can combine lower-pressure individual learning with broader relationships and facilities. A disadvantage is that changing between settings can increase anxiety and sensory load when routines or adjustments are inconsistent.

AI, SCHOOLING & PARENT CHOICE

Would an AI robot teacher make families more or less likely to choose school?

The Salamanca humanoid robot pilot

In July 2026, Guardian reporting described a planned pilot at Salamanca High School in New York involving a humanoid robot called Sally and an AI teaching assistant. Supporters promoted personalised learning and supervised exposure to emerging technology, while teachers, community members and the New York State Education Department raised concerns about privacy, safeguards, cultural sensitivity and functions that should remain with trained professionals.

For students with high support needs, AI could be useful when it provides repetition, accessible formats, communication support or practice at an individual pace. The ethical problem begins when a system is treated as a substitute for relationships, professional judgement or accountability. Students whose wellbeing depends on trusted adults, careful observation and responsive adjustments may be particularly affected if “personalisation” is reduced to an algorithm rather than a teacher knowing the student.

This could influence parent schooling choices in both directions. Some families may prefer a school or hybrid setting if AI gives their child better accessibility while qualified teachers remain responsible. Other parents may become more interested in home schooling if they believe classroom technology is replacing human contact, collecting too much data or being introduced without meaningful consultation. Northern Pathways would therefore use AI only as an optional learning tool, with a person remaining responsible for teaching, wellbeing and decisions.
